17 scientists. 17 men. Here’s what I’m doing about it

Quick question for my fellow chemistry teachers: how many women scientists does the IAL Chemistry specification name?

I counted. The answer is zero. 😬

There are 17 named scientists in there, from Avogadro to Grignard, and every single one is a man. The International GCSE is even sparser: it names exactly one person, Le Chatelier, and only to say students don’t need to know about him!

None of us wrote the spec. But we’re the ones in front of the class, so we get to decide what students actually hear. And no, this isn’t a plea to squeeze in a whole new unit. Everything below takes minutes, not lessons.

So what did I find?

It all started with a new study from the University of Cambridge in October 2026. The researchers counted every scientist named in the science specifications of the UK’s six main exam boards, Pearson Edexcel included (the paper; press release). Here’s what they found:

  • GCSE level: 86 scientists named, one woman (Rosalind Franklin), and she appeared in a single specification.
  • A level: 181 scientists named, three women (Rosalind Franklin, Marie Tharp and Inge Lehmann).

That study looked at UK GCSEs and A levels, not the international courses so many of us teach. So I grabbed the Pearson specs and counted myself, including the names hiding inside terms like “Hess’s law”:

The IAL result: 17 men, 0 women. Avogadro, Hess, Born, Haber, Maxwell, Boltzmann, Brønsted, Lowry, Arrhenius, Benedict, Fehling, Tollens, Harcourt, Esson, Friedel, Crafts and Grignard.

And it’s not just the specs. A 2022 study of popular UK and Irish chemistry textbooks found one book naming 48 men and 2 women, and another naming 45 men and 1 woman (the study).

In fairness, I counted from the PDFs, so I may have missed a name or two. And the spec isn’t the textbook, which names more people. If you spot one I missed, let me know!

Why should we care?

The Cambridge team think this could be one reason girls drop STEM subjects at 16 more often than boys. Students decide who “does” chemistry long before they pick their A levels, and a list of 17 men is part of that picture.

It’s also just... not accurate! Women did work that sits right inside our courses: proving the benzene ring is flat, building early gas chromatographs, discovering a Group 1 metal. Our students learn the chemistry and never hear who found it.

And our classrooms are international. Chemists from China, India and the Americas give lots of our students someone who looks like them, not just another European man from the 1800s. 🌍

Let’s be honest about time

I know, I know. The IAL content is enormous. Six units, core practicals, mocks, and a spring term that vanishes every single year. Nobody has time for a “women in chemistry” project week, and a lesson on history is a lesson not spent on equilibria.

So here’s the trick: don’t add anything. Just change the names, examples and stories you already use. We tell students dozens of little stories every year anyway, in starters, worked examples, exam questions and wall displays. Swapping some of them costs almost nothing.

7 quick wins (minutes, not lessons)

  1. Name her when you teach it. Hitting chromatography, benzene or catalysts? Add one sentence: who did this, and when. Thirty seconds, tops.
  2. Sneak her onto your starter slide. Add a small photo and one line to your usual Do Now or retrieval quiz. Same activity, different background.
  3. Set your questions in her lab. Writing a calculation or data question anyway? Use a real woman’s experiment as the context. Students practise exactly the same skill.
  4. Make the hypothetical chemist a “she”. “A chemist measured the rate; she found...” It costs nothing, and students notice more than you’d think.
  5. Change your walls once. One afternoon on a display works for the whole year. Even better, hand it to a keen sixth-former as a project.
  6. Share the load with your department. One person adds a scientist to each topic’s shared slides, and everyone else just teaches from them.
  7. Tell Pearson! The new IAL Chemistry spec starts in September 2027. Feedback from the teachers who deliver it matters, and an email takes five minutes.

One small warning: try not to make it a big announcement (“and now... a FEMALE scientist!”). The goal is for her to just be there, as a normal part of the chemistry. 👩‍🔬

Look beyond Europe, too 🌍

While we’re at it, let’s widen the map as well as the mix of men and women. Nearly every name in the spec is European, but chemistry has always been global. Lots of us teach in international schools, where students come from all over the world, so this matters just as much.

Here are some favourites that slot straight into IAL topics:

  • al-Razi (Persia, c. 900) distilled crude oil and described his apparatus over a thousand years ago. Perfect for fractional distillation.
  • Ahmed Zewail (Egypt) used ultra-fast lasers to watch bonds break in real time, and won the 1999 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. A great hook for rates and transition states.
  • Mario Molina (Mexico) showed that CFCs destroy the ozone layer, and shared the 1995 Nobel Prize. Ideal for radical reactions of halogenoalkanes.
  • Ryoji Noyori (Japan) made catalysts that produce just one optical isomer of a drug. Nobel Prize 2001, and a perfect link to chirality.
  • Akira Yoshino (Japan) developed the lithium-ion battery in your students’ phones. Nobel Prize 2019, for electrochemistry.
  • Hideki Shirakawa (Japan) made a plastic that conducts electricity. Nobel Prize 2000, for polymers.
  • Omar Yaghi (born in Jordan) builds metal-organic frameworks, crystals full of tiny holes that can pull water out of desert air. Nobel Prize 2025, for transition metal complexes.
  • Andrés Manuel del Río (Mexico) discovered vanadium in 1801, and the Elhuyar brothers (Spain) first isolated tungsten in 1783. Both fit transition metals.
  • Asima Chatterjee (India) and Tu Youyou (China) from the table above, too!

The point isn’t to replace the Europeans. Hess and Arrhenius still matter! It’s about letting every student see that people like them have done this chemistry.

Over to you

You don’t need to do all of this. Just pick your next topic, find its row in the table, and add one sentence and one photo to your slides. Keep that up all year and your students will meet a dozen women chemists, without you losing a single lesson.

The spec has 17 men and no women. Our lessons don’t have to look the same. 💪
