What's happening

Key points

  • An addition polymer is made when many small molecules called monomers join together to make one very long molecule (the polymer).
  • The monomers are alkenes. One bond of the C=C double bond opens and the monomers join with new C–C single bonds. The polymer is saturated, and it is the only product.
  • Repeat unit: change C=C to C–C, draw a bond out from each of these carbons, put brackets across those bonds and write n. Side groups (H, CH3, Cl, F) stay on the same carbon.
  • Monomer from a repeat unit: remove the brackets, n and the extension bonds, then put the C=C back between the two carbons.
  • Names: ethene → poly(ethene); propene → poly(propene); chloroethene → poly(chloroethene), PVC; tetrafluoroethene → poly(tetrafluoroethene), PTFE.
  • Disposal problems: addition polymers are inert and non-biodegradable, so they stay in landfill for hundreds of years. Burning them gives toxic gases, e.g. carbon monoxide (incomplete combustion) and hydrogen chloride (from PVC).
  • Recycling: used polymers are collected, sorted by type, washed, shredded, melted and moulded into new products. It reduces landfill, saves crude oil (a finite resource) and energy, and avoids toxic gases from burning. But sorting and cleaning cost money and energy, and the quality drops each time.
